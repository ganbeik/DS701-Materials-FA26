\begin{solutionbox}

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{(\alph{enumi})}
\item
  \textbf{Model A:} \(\bar y = (7+8+8+9)/4 = 8\); bias \(= 8-10 = -2\),
  bias\(^2 = 4\); variance \(= (1+0+0+1)/4 = 0.5\). \textbf{Model B:}
  \(\bar y = (6+14+8+12)/4 = 10\); bias \(= 0\), bias\(^2 = 0\);
  variance \(= (16+16+4+4)/4 = 10\). A is high bias (systematically 2
  below the truth) but low variance (predictions barely move between
  training sets); B is unbiased on average but high variance (its
  prediction swings from 6 to 14 depending on which training set it
  saw).
\item
  Expected squared error: A: \(4 + 0.5 + 3 = 7.5\); B:
  \(0 + 10 + 3 = 13\). Deploy A --- its systematic error costs less than
  B's instability. The choice does not depend on \(\sigma^2\): the noise
  term is added equally to both, so it never changes which is smaller
  (\(4.5\) vs \(10\) before noise). \emph{Common mistake:} concluding B
  is better because its average prediction is exactly right --- a single
  deployed model is one draw, not the average over training sets.
\item
  \textbf{X is high bias:} training and validation error converge
  quickly to about \(4\) and then plateau together (gap \(\approx 0.1\)
  at \(n = 5000\)). More data will not help --- the two curves have
  already met; the model cannot represent the pattern. Remedy: a more
  flexible model (more/other features, higher-order terms, less
  regularization). \textbf{Y is high variance:} training error is near
  zero throughout while validation error is far above it, and the gap is
  still closing at \(n = 5000\) (\(0.4\) vs \(3.0\)). Here more data
  \emph{is} worth it (validation error is still falling steeply); other
  remedies from Lecture 07 are limiting model complexity via a
  hyperparameter or adding regularization.
\end{enumerate}

\end{solutionbox}

A lender records, for ten past borrowers, whether they own their home,
whether they are married, and whether they later \textbf{defaulted} (the

With \(p_i\) the fraction of class \(i\) at a node (and
\(0\log_2 0 = 0\)), use
\[\text{Entropy}=-\sum_i p_i\log_2 p_i,\qquad \text{Gini}=1-\sum_i p_i^2 .\]

\(I(\text{children})=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{N(v_j)}{N}\,I(v_j)\), and the
information gain of a split is \(I(\text{parent})-I(\text{children})\).
You may use \(\log_2 3 \approx 1.585\) and \(\log_2 5 \approx 2.322\);
every other logarithm you need follows from these
(e.g.~\(\log_2\tfrac{2}{5} = 1 - \log_2 5\)). Round to 3 decimals.

\end{enumerate}

\begin{solutionbox}

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{(\alph{enumi})}
\item
  Root: 4 Default = Y, 6 Default = N, so \(p = (0.4, 0.6)\).
  \(\log_2 0.4 = 1 - 2.322 = -1.322\),
  \(\log_2 0.6 = 1.585 - 2.322 = -0.737\). Entropy
  \(= -(0.4)(-1.322) - (0.6)(-0.737) = 0.529 + 0.442 = 0.971\). Gini
  \(= 1 - 0.16 - 0.36 = 0.480\).
\item
  \textbf{Home owner.} Y: rows 1--4, (0 Y, 4 N): entropy 0, Gini 0. N:
  rows 5--10, (4 Y, 2 N), \(p = (\tfrac23, \tfrac13)\):
  \(\log_2\tfrac23 = -0.585\), \(\log_2\tfrac13 = -1.585\); entropy
  \(= 0.390 + 0.528 = 0.918\); Gini
  \(= 1 - \tfrac49 - \tfrac19 = 0.444\). Collective entropy
  \(= 0.4(0) + 0.6(0.918) = 0.551\); gain \(= 0.971 - 0.551 = 0.420\).
  Weighted Gini \(= 0.6(0.444) = 0.267\); Gini gain \(= 0.213\).
  \textbf{Married.} Y: rows 1, 2, 5, 6, 7, (1 Y, 4 N),
  \(p = (0.2, 0.8)\): \(\log_2 0.2 = -2.322\), \(\log_2 0.8 = -0.322\);
  entropy \(= 0.464 + 0.258 = 0.722\); Gini
  \(= 1 - 0.04 - 0.64 = 0.320\). N: rows 3, 4, 8, 9, 10, (3 Y, 2 N):
  entropy \(0.971\), Gini \(0.480\) (same proportions as the root,
  reversed). Collective entropy \(= 0.5(0.722) + 0.5(0.971) = 0.846\);
  gain \(= 0.125\). Weighted Gini \(= 0.5(0.320) + 0.5(0.480) = 0.400\);
  Gini gain \(= 0.080\). Choose \textbf{Home owner} (gain 0.420 vs
  0.125); Gini agrees (0.213 vs 0.080).
\item
  The impure child is Home owner = N (rows 5--10, entropy 0.918). Split
  on Married: Married = Y: rows 5, 6, 7, (1 Y, 2 N), entropy
  \(= 0.918\); Married = N: rows 8, 9, 10, (3 Y, 0 N), entropy 0.
  Collective \(= 0.5(0.918) + 0 = 0.459\); gain
  \(= 0.918 - 0.459 = 0.459\). Tree: Home owner = Y \(\to\) predict N;
  Home owner = N and Married = N \(\to\) predict Y; Home owner = N and
  Married = Y \(\to\) (1 Y, 2 N) majority N. Training accuracy: only row
  5 is misclassified, so \(9/10 = 90\%\) (the depth-1 stump gets
  \(8/10\)).
\item
  To make the (1 Y, 2 N) leaf pure the algorithm needs some attribute on
  which row 5 differs from rows 6 and 7 --- with enough attributes
  (income, age, zip code, \ldots) one always exists, so a fully grown
  tree carves out a leaf for every stray training row. Those tiny leaves
  record noise, not pattern; a different training sample would produce
  different leaves, which is exactly high variance.
  \texttt{max\_depth\ =\ 1} stops after the Home owner split: the stump
  with \(8/10\) training accuracy, higher bias but more stable.
  \texttt{min\_samples\_leaf\ =\ 3} permits the tree in (c) (every leaf
  has 3 or 4 rows) but forbids any further split of the (1 Y, 2 N) leaf,
  since it would create a leaf with fewer than 3 rows --- the guard
  bites exactly at the noisy leaf.
\end{enumerate}

\end{solutionbox}

  \(\operatorname{Var}(X+Y) = \operatorname{Var}X + \operatorname{Var}Y + 2\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)\),
  \(\operatorname{Var}(aX) = a^2\operatorname{Var}X\), and
  \(\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y) = \rho\,\sigma^2\) when both variances equal
  \(\sigma^2\).
\end{enumerate}

\answerspace{2in}
\answerpage

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{(\alph{enumi})}
\setcounter{enumi}{2}
\tightlist
\item
  \emph{Bagging} grows each tree on a bootstrap sample of the rows and
  averages; a \emph{random forest} does the same but, at every split,
  considers only a random subset of \texttt{max\_features} of the
  attributes. In terms of \(\rho\), \(\sigma^2\), and \(B\), say what
  each ingredient buys, and why the L08 activity found that bagging
  alone (all features at every split) barely improved on one deep tree
  when a few strong attributes dominate. What might a very small
  \texttt{max\_features} cost you?
\end{enumerate}

\answerspace{2.2in}

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{(\alph{enumi})}
\setcounter{enumi}{3}
\tightlist
\item
  \emph{Boosting} (not covered in lecture --- here is all you need) fits
  many \emph{shallow} trees in sequence, each one trained on the errors
  the ensemble has made so far, and adds them up. Which of bias and
  variance is a shallow tree short on, so which term is boosting mainly
  reducing? Contrast with the forest in one sentence: why deep trees for
  a forest but shallow trees for boosting?
\end{enumerate}

\answerspace{1.8in}

\begin{solutionbox}

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{(\alph{enumi})}
\item
  \((0.8, 10)\): \(0.8 + 0.2/10 = 0.820\). \((0.8, 100)\):
  \(0.8 + 0.2/100 = 0.802\). \((0.2, 100)\): \(0.2 + 0.8/100 = 0.208\).
  As \(B \to \infty\) the second term vanishes and the variance tends to
  \(\rho\sigma^2\): \(0.8\) and \(0.2\) respectively. Going from 10 to
  100 trees at \(\rho = 0.8\) shaved \(0.018\); dropping \(\rho\) from
  \(0.8\) to \(0.2\) cut the variance by four --- decorrelating the
  trees is the lever, since the \(\rho\sigma^2\) floor cannot be
  averaged away.
\item
  \(\operatorname{Var}\big(\tfrac12(X+Y)\big) = \tfrac14(\sigma^2 + \sigma^2 + 2\rho\sigma^2)
  = \tfrac{\sigma^2(1+\rho)}{2}\). And
  \(\rho\sigma^2 + \tfrac{(1-\rho)\sigma^2}{2} = \tfrac{2\rho\sigma^2 + \sigma^2 - \rho\sigma^2}{2}
  = \tfrac{\sigma^2(1+\rho)}{2}\). Equal.
\item
  Bagging attacks the \((1-\rho)\sigma^2/B\) term: averaging \(B\)
  noisy, low-bias deep trees divides the \emph{uncorrelated} part of
  their variance by \(B\). But bootstrap samples of the same data still
  let the same few strong attributes win the top splits, so the trees
  look alike --- \(\rho\) is high, the \(\rho\sigma^2\) floor is high,
  and (as in (a)) more trees barely help. \texttt{max\_features} attacks
  \(\rho\): forcing splits to consider random attribute subsets makes
  different trees use different attributes, decorrelating them and
  lowering the floor --- the ingredient that made the forest beat plain
  bagging. Cost: each tree is denied its best split more often, so its
  own error (bias, and a bit of \(\sigma^2\)) rises; too small a subset
  and the trees are individually poor --- which is why
  \texttt{max\_features} is tuned (about \(\sqrt{p}\) by default).
\item
  A shallow tree (a stump or depth 2--3) is high bias, low variance: it
  is too simple to fit the pattern but is stable. Boosting adds trees
  that each correct what the current sum still gets wrong, so the sum
  becomes progressively more flexible --- it reduces \textbf{bias}. A
  forest starts from low-bias, high-variance deep trees and averages
  away variance; boosting starts from high-bias, low-variance shallow
  trees and adds up bias-reducing corrections. Averaging shallow trees
  would fix nothing (their shared error is bias, and averaging cannot
  remove bias); boosting deep trees would have little bias left to
  remove and would overfit fast.
\end{enumerate}

\end{solutionbox}
